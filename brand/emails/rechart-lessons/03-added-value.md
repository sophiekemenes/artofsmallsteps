Hi {{Nickname}},

I'm really curious what you've found, if you've done the last exercises.

So let's sum up where we are. We're still at the very beginning of the road towards our goals, and we've only pulled the cover off a few of the tools in your toolbox. But if you've been playing along with me these past few days, you'll have noticed two things already:

- the downstairs neighbour, your subconscious, has lots and lots of unknown things in its many rooms that you can use as resources
- the way you relate to the world goes from the inside outwards

These two experiences will always be very useful to you. Shall we add a third?

We're staying in the same territory, and getting to know the idea of **anchoring.** If we can agree that those filters, those submodalities, can easily change your mood and your opinion, then we won't argue much about anchors either.

The theory itself isn't some brand-new invention, because it's based on observing something that already exists:

certain circumstances, smells, physical sensations can trigger a reaction in you, instantly and obviously.

A few situations with physical symptoms you'll know too: the classic Pavlov reflex, where the dogs start salivating at the sound of the bell.

But maybe it's even simpler if I say: imagine biting into a lemon. (Sorry.)

Or imagine someone dragging their nails down a blackboard. (Sorry again!)

Out of the three, at least one has very probably given you a physical symptom too. :)

But let's wipe our mouths and shake off the goosebumps, because the question is: how will this knowledge help us knock things into shape?

Well, these kinds of automatic reflexes don't only work physically. They work with emotions, moods and states of mind too.

You know the formula. You work in such a way that you react to something in some way. The best part is that these two somethings can be ANYTHING. Anything you choose. A memory, a physical touch, and if it's anchored properly, the feeling you linked to it turns up.

Think of the possibilities, though! When you're not feeling great, when you could do with a bit of confidence, you just touch your wrist at a certain spot, and slowly the feeling you were longing for washes through you again?

Of course, for it to work well, certain conditions need to be met. The next letter will be about that.

But for now, before that, I'd like to invite you on a little exploration again, because no theory can replace learning through your own personal experience.

## Task 1 · The joy detector

And while we're exploring, why not go looking for something good? Your task is very simple. Over the course of tomorrow, right from the moment you open your eyes to the world, consciously try to catch in the act every thing, happening and event that brings you joy.

You don't need to go hunting for some fantastic, fan-girling whoop of delight. We're looking for little joys, little satisfactions, and where they come from.

It could be your coffee, or the smell of the top of your child's head. It could fill you up when someone holds the door for you, or kindly lets you go first at the lights. When someone touches your shoulder in a certain spot. When, say, after standing around for ages, you can finally sit down. When you step into the shower after a workout, pleasantly tired.

Anything goes! Everything counts.

And with every single joy, try to catch which part of your body you feel it in, and what you feel, when you feel joy! Do your ears tingle? (Why not? It's perfectly possible.) Does a pleasant warmth start up in your stomach? Or the way your lips stretch over your teeth when you smile, say?

Notice the connections! What are the buttons that press the joy in you? I'm sure you already know lots of them, but it can be surprising how many extra mini joy-factors show up at times like this.

As well as being something we'll keep working with during the course, this can be a useful part of everyday life all by itself. Because if you know where that button is, what's stopping you from pressing it?

What's more, when you've got to the end of the day, I've got something else for you.

## Task 2 · Pen Companion

I've already mentioned why it's important that we write the exercises down. But we haven't talked about the pen yet. Now we will!

Because later on, this pen is going to appear in our story like a magic wand.

Of course, of course, in the stories wizards don't necessarily need a wand, since the magic is in them, but a good tool can be the focus of their magic.

And soon we'll get to the point where we bring back the less and less used habit of writing by hand, and take it into our own creative goal-setting toolbox.

And for that, dear {{Nickname}}, it doesn't hurt to have a special pen. Well, not special in itself, but special to us.

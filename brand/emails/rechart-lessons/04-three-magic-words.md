Hi {{Nickname}},

how are you today?
And your pen? :)

You know, I've been thinking: even if I give you tasks that you might enjoy and be able to use… as long as I don't tell you what they're for, and what's behind them, it may not be that easy to focus.

So before I ask how the joy detecting went the other day, I owe you a bit of an explanation.

Setting goals is important, and a great thing. The dreams we want to reach, our longings, the secret sighs of our hearts floating in front of us, are seriously and concretely essential for living our everyday lives, and not just existing. That's how we get ahead across the bog, hopping from stepping stone to stepping stone. By knowing what we want. And knowing who we are (or at least having a hunch, and being able to make peace with it).

Do you know the five biggest regrets of the dying? An Australian palliative care nurse, Bronnie Ware, wrote down what her patients told her in their last weeks. I'll admit, it made a big impression on me. Because what could be a bigger motivating force than someone looking back over the whole course of their life and realising what, in their heart of hearts, they would have done differently?

I really do believe in learning from our own mistakes (so much that I've just been thinking I could write about that separately, but no, no, I'll stay on the right track), because there's no greater teacher than your own experience. But at the same time it's foolish to turn down other people's insights when they come from three times as much life lived as our own.

We won't go through them all, just this one:

"I wish I'd had the courage to live a life true to myself, not the life others expected of me."

A very strong sentence, isn't it?
And I'll bet anything you know exactly what inspired it. And that in your life, too, there are countless things like this that you give up for something else, for someone else. Family, society, work, the future, do I need to go on?

This sentence made me think quite a lot. It's the biggest regret of the dying. And it's not a good thought to leave the world with.

But if you're thinking that I'm suggesting the opposite, you're wrong.
Somehow I don't think a lot of people who put only their own interests first end up happier at the end of their lives.

So what's the answer?
Well. We talked about it just the other day.
You can look at the same life in more than one way, through more than one filter. And it's a bit wild that this, in fact, is what separates contentment from regret.

So during the course we'll get to the point where we ask the question, and walk all the way round it: who are we, and what do we want? I'll show you the best goal-setting methods.

But why do we take fairly big detours at the start… well, exactly because of this. The ego, identity and goals, all of these are needed for a full life.
But our everyday lives, thank you very much, sometimes think otherwise, and don't hesitate to throw grey, nasty barriers in front of us, and in front of the courage we could live our own lives with.

That's why I'm stuffing your pockets with playful exercises. Because, as we know, play is the most serious thing in the world, and it's what we learn the most from.

That's why, before you take on the biggest, most exciting projects of your life and start setting goals, I'd like you first to be on good terms with yourself.

That's why I'm giving you a look at a few simple methods. I can't do them for you, but if you do them, they prepare you inside for the life-changing work that will make the difference in how you look back on your life, and especially on this year. With regret, or with contentment.

And while you'll obviously need courage for it, "little things" like words can be useful too.

## Three life-changing words

Yes, yes, words. Our language is the tool we express our thoughts with, and, as the earlier exercises have already hinted, it works both ways. What you say, the way you put things, feeds back into your thinking. Your thinking feeds back into your behaviour. Your behaviour into your surroundings. And so on.

If we reshape the sentence above a little, this is what we can hear in it:
I couldn't live my own life, because I did what others expected.
What I had to.
What I had to. What I must. It wasn't my fault, it had to be done.

The first word to rewrite inside ourselves is

**Have to**

Ouch, right? That pesky "have to"? The "must"? When some outside circumstance completely beyond us forces us against our better intentions? How does that make us feel?
It's really important, at every "have to" moment, to stop for a second and refine what it means.

Because how did the situation where you "must" do something come about?
You made a decision earlier, and you chose something. Whether well or badly is beside the point here: you were the one who acted. Today's "have to" situation is the continuation of an earlier one, something you chose. Yes, now this run has to be finished, but later you can draw the lessons, and choose differently.

This isn't an easy word to change, but I say: go for it. Take the control back. Even a tiny bit of taking responsibility like this can give you so much strength! You don't need to wait for the end of the road. You can do it today.

What to use instead? I've chosen to do this (and I may be dying to be able to do it differently.)

**Try**

Master Yoda didn't like this word, and you know, I came to something similar when I started paying attention to how people talk about themselves: the word "try" always seems to smuggle the possibility of failure in with it, so I avoid it myself, even in the way I speak day to day.
If you try to do something, there's an inner assumption that it won't work out at all.

And we wouldn't want to be secretly sabotaging ourselves before we've even started anything, would we? So take this one playfully!

What to use instead? Test. Experiment. Even "set out to find out" is gentler than "try". Be creative, you know your own way of speaking best. Let there be a different action in it. Because before you do something, you check, with what you know now, whether it works, and that way you gain new experience. That's not trying, which can end in yes or no. It's a test, with any result at all. And once you've tested it, you act.

**Problem**

Oh dear. Surely I'm not trying to spoil things, or question that problems really do exist? Wouldn't dream of it!
But I'd like you to refine the meaning attached to it.

Most of the time, a problem lives in our heads as some impenetrable, mountain-sized monster that sits on us and wraps itself around us. Invisibly, lurking, the idea of helplessness sticks to the word "problem". So here we are again. The control isn't ours. In fact, the more we chew a problem over, the more it tends to bulk up, and even if it started as a flea, all that overthinking makes it swell into a hippo.
And yet we do usually solve problems, don't we? Hm… so… what if we simply used the word "task" instead? We usually solve tasks too. Not that big a difference, is it? Lots of people already see a problem as a task, but as with the other words, what matters here is how you've been doing it so far.

There's no written exercise with today's material, and maybe that's exactly what makes it harder. Because instead, I'd like you to watch, strictly and caringly, how many times these words (have to, try, problem) come up in the way you speak every day, and when you'd use them, well… use something else instead!
Our goal now is to change the direction of our thinking. Let's say we're putting a filter on our words. And just like then, we won't be lying with it, because we haven't changed the original.

The first few times you realise you're using them, notice it and do something about it, you'll very probably feel a subtle mental resistance, because you're "interfering" with the usual rhythm of how your mind works. (And if it turns out to be surprisingly easy for you, then it's "suspicious" that your mind was already longing for a change like this. :)

What can you expect from this?
I won't tell you in advance. :)
I'd like you to experience it.

Oh, and of course we're not banishing the poor words forever. Let's face it, it would be very odd if, say, in a dark cellar, you weren't allowed to say "There must be a light switch here somewhere…". Or you'd easily confuse the person on your broadband provider's helpline if you said "Hello, I'm {{Nickname}}, and I've got a little task with my router." (Not to mention "Houston, we have a task.")

Change the words where and when they're about you, and could affect life tasks, moods, motivation. You don't "have to" do this, you know… but since you're here, you've already chosen it a little.

All the best until next time!

With love,
Sophie

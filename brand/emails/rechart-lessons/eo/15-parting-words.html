We haven't said much about NLP presuppositions in these letters, though we could have. They're statements we don't try to explain, prove or take apart. We simply decide to accept them as true.

One of them goes like this:

A person is not their behaviour.

That's a useful sentence when you want to be more understanding towards other people. But today I want to put the weight on something else: the times when you stick labels on yourself, based on what you've done.

I am who I am. I do what I do.

Negative thoughts and labels do the most damage when they become part of your picture of yourself. Especially when you're the one who puts them there.

You drop a glass: I'm so clumsy.
You get lost in your own neighbourhood: I'm an idiot.
You flirt with someone, and they turn you down: I'm a loser.
And if it all happens on the same day: I'm a complete disaster.

Of course, someone with a basically healthy mind can identify with the opposite, positive feelings in just the same way. But I'm not giving away any big secret if I say that we always remember the negative things better, and they tend to hit us harder, don't they?

Not long ago, I was at a meeting, and looking back, I really wasn't my most wonderful self. It added up from little things: I was tired, my shoulder hurt and I could barely move it, and I wasn't really in the mood for being at a self-development event just then. At times like that I can be pretty grumpy, and I know it, just as I know it shows on me. So I try to inflict it on as few people as possible, and to make my exit as soon as I can. Politeness gets harder. I need a bit of distance to pull myself together.

A dear acquaintance caught me right in the middle of one of these exits. I tried to explain, in a few words, why I wasn't quite myself, and they replied: "Fine, then go home, feel sorry for yourself and lick your wounds."

Lovely, isn't it? :) The perfect recipe for having one person fewer in your life. Communication gone completely wrong, if you put my grumpy behaviour next to their not exactly kind reaction.

But this is exactly when it shines out, and when it matters to remember: a person is not their behaviour. Not me with mine, and not them with theirs. Words said and things done can't be taken back. They leave a mark on the fabric of time, a stain or a seal. It happened, that's that. But it makes a big difference how we look at them.

You'll probably never be at your best all the time. You'll still be grumpy, rude, silly, jealous, envious. In your downstairs neighbour's million cupboards, a million programmes are busy noticing things and producing answers, and pushing you into reactions you'd never choose consciously. You'll still get overwhelmed, take offence, have had enough.

However.

Whenever you do something you're later ashamed of, would undo if you could, or wish you'd never done, NEVER put an equals sign between that and yourself.

You're not stupid. You did something stupid.
You're not an idiot. The right answer just didn't come to you in that moment.
You're not a coward. You just weren't in a resourceful enough state, right then, to act differently.

Never you. Always what you did.

It's so much easier to forgive yourself for a wrong action than for being wrong yourself.

There is no failure, only feedback

And that brings me to my other favourite NLP presupposition:

There is no failure, only feedback.

Not an easy sentence. But next to the first one, the two make a beautiful pair.

Because a wrong action is very easy to file away later as a mistake. And on top of that, we've been trained for it. Since we were small, both school and society have loved to treat mistakes and failures as something that defines us. As children we learn that mistakes make us worse than others, because we get worse grades, and our parents don't love us as much because of them. (Obviously. Come home with top grades and they're delighted. Come home with a bad one and they're beside themselves.)

That belief, that deep groove, isn't easy to break. Shame about mistakes has already caused far too much hurt for far too many people.

Listen, {{Nickname|default("Wayfinder")}}.

What you call a mistake was the very best you could do, given everything you were at that moment: your state, your emotional maturity, your physical and mental development, everything around you.

Back then, you did the best you could.

There's a reason you decided what you decided, and did what you did. Today you're so much more than that. In fact, two minutes after the "mistake" you were already more. And with every moment that passes in which you look at the feedback the situation gave you, and something changes in you because of it, you become more still.

Every navigator knows this. No boat sails in a perfectly straight line. Wind and current push it off course all the time, and the navigator corrects, and corrects, and corrects again. Nobody calls that failing. It's called sailing.

The things we call mistakes are the proof that we're moving, doing, and daring.

Look at them that way!

Your last task

Open your notebook, and look back through a few pages from your free writing. Circle every place where you talked about yourself in a negative way. Highlight every "I'm bad" sentence, and on a fresh page, rewrite them.

Use these verbs, one way or another:

  • I did...
  • I reacted...
  • I assumed...
  • I acted...

Separate the behaviour from the identity, and soon you'll start to feel the difference.

And if, later on, you catch yourself sticking a label on yourself because of something you did, rewrite it as soon as you can. You'll see how much weight it takes off your picture of yourself.

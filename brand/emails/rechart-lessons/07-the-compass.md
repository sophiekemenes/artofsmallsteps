Hi {{Nickname}},

Paper ready? Pen Companion within reach? Lovely. Today we start drawing.

But first, a small question that turns out to be the foundation of everything that follows.

What does it actually mean to set a goal? Roughly: you think it through and plan what you want to get to. True, but so general it's almost useless. Let me put it more simply: a goal says **what change you want in your life.**

Change is the engine. Not just in goal-setting, in life in general. We'd all fall asleep watching Star Wars if Luke Skywalker just stood there on the screen for two hours.

But a goal is more than change. Luke doesn't become a moisture farmer, or a cleaner. He becomes a Jedi. That's a change in a direction that means something to him (even if the Empire strikes back).

Now, you might quite rightly point out that you have no wish to become a Jedi, and you don't owe me an explanation. If you'd rather be a ninja, a superhero or, heaven forbid, a marine biologist, you have my blessing. Because this is exactly where we have to start: **what's important to you.** Until you know that, you can't really know what you want. With years of hard work you can become almost anything. But what if that anything doesn't bring you any joy at all?

So before any destination, before any route, every navigator needs the one instrument that never lies to them: a compass. And your compass is made of your values.

## What's a value?

Answer me this:

**What are the most important things in your life?**

Could you name at least five?

These are what we call values. They're called that because they matter, they're worth something to you.

It sounds like an easy question, and I can tell you why it isn't.

There are general values, the ones we could also call social expectations: money, a career, a big car, a nice house, a happy family, a flat stomach, confident presence, the latest phone. That doesn't make them bad. It just means they matter to a lot of people, and they may or may not matter to you.

And when someone answers in their own words, they might say: I want to always have enough money, and I never want to be ill. Let's stay with those two for a moment. They're two different directions. "Enough money" is about what I want to have. "Never being ill" is about what I want to avoid.

Both count, both work. The first is a **towards** value (you want to bring it closer). The second is an **away from** value (you can guess why).

## Task 1 · At least ten

List at least ten important things in your life. What matters to you? They can be things you want (creating security) and things you want to avoid (avoiding uncertainty).

You can write fifty if you like. There's no upper limit, only a lower one: ten.

If you're stuck, blank, or would simply like some examples, here are 330 towards values [VALUES-LIST-APPROACH] and 165 away-from values [VALUES-LIST-AVOID]. There are many more, of course, but these two lists are plenty to wake up your creative side.

Got your ten? Then read on.

## Task 2 · Five cards

Already reading on? Good. Now please make some little cards, out of paper. If you cut an ordinary sheet into eight pieces, that's about right. You'll need at least ten, so cut two.

Write one value on each card.

Then spread the cards out in front of you, and put them in order of priority. It's easier if you start by picking out the five most important, or by taking away the five least important. Then work with just five. The most important goes at the top. The second most important underneath. And so on.

If, say, your work, dancing, your friends, a lovely home and travel are important to you, it might look like this:

1. work
2. a lovely home
3. travel
4. friends
5. dancing

Or like this:

1. friends
2. work
3. dancing
4. a lovely home
5. travel

Or a hundred other ways.

**If you get stuck, use the bubble method.** Imagine that the more important something is, the bigger its bubble. The bigger bubble rises to the top and pushes the smaller one, the less important one, underneath.

1. Choose the five that are in the running. Put the rest aside.
2. Put them in order by your first instinct.
3. Go down the whole line like this: compare the first two, the top one and the one under it. If the top one really is more important, leave it. If not, swap them. Then move one step down and compare the second and the third. Right order? Leave it. Wrong? Swap. Keep going until you reach the bottom.
4. Repeat step 3 as many times as you need, until nothing has to move any more. That's your order.

Number the cards, and put them in an envelope. (Take a photo of them first, if you're as digital as I am. Most of us are these days.) The envelope doesn't need a stamp or an address, but please write this on it: **"My values – [today's date]"**. The date matters.

Have you put the cards in the envelope? Yes? Great.

Now please take them out again. We're not finished, what's the hurry?

## Task 3 · How full is each one?

Take the first card and read what's on it. This is the most important thing in your life.

Think about how present it is in your life right now.

Then colour it in. Take a coloured pencil and shade as much of the card as shows how satisfied you are with this value at the moment.

If the card says "love", and your sweetheart is kissing the back of your neck right now, can't wait for you to finish this exercise, you can shade the whole thing, pop it in the envelope and go and jump into their arms.

If the card says "love", but the last time you had anything like that kind of joy, body and soul, was three years ago, then shade just a thin strip.

Do the same with all five.

Don't put them back yet. First, take out your wish list from lesson 5.

## Task 4 · Wishes that fit

Go through your wish list, and for every wish, longing or future goal that fits one of your core values, either:

a) write it on a separate card and put it in the envelope, or
b) write it on the back of the card of the value it belongs to.

If one of your cards says "adventure", for example, because adventure matters to you, then the "trek across Africa" on your wish list can go with it.

The wishes that don't fit any of your values: leave them aside for now. Not thrown away. Just not on this voyage.

## What the compass is for

Let me show you why this matters so much, with a made-up example.

Say the top three on your list are:

1. people, relationships
2. making music
3. money

Right now your relationships aren't very rich. You don't go out much, you don't see your friends often. If you had to put a number on it, you'd say 10%.

You're half happy with your music. You play the piano at home regularly, but it would be nice to show other people what you can do. 50%.

Money's not a big problem. You don't earn badly and you don't need much right now. 70%.

You don't feel great in your own skin, because the most important thing, your relationships, is in quite a bad way.

And then you get an offer: an online job, double your current pay, for one and a half times the hours, and you wouldn't even have to go into an office.

If you just say yes (and there are plenty of good reasons to), you'd make your life worse. More money, which you were already fairly happy with. Much less time. You'd see even fewer people than before, so your relationships would get poorer still, and there'd probably be less time for music too.

You'd give up your number one and number two for your number three. With the compass in your hand, you'd say no to that offer. Or at least you'd say yes knowing exactly what it costs.

That's the compass. Whenever you have a decision to make, you can ask: **which choice brings my core values closer, and which one takes me further away?**

## Your map · Layer 1: the compass rose

And now, the first line on your map.

Take your big sheet. In one corner, the way old maps do it, draw a compass rose, and give it five points, one for each of your top values.

At the top, at north, write your number one. The value you'll always steer by. Then the others around it, clockwise, in order.

Now colour each point, as far as you shaded its card. Some will be full. Some will be barely a sliver. Both are honest, and both are useful: the slivers show you where the wind is missing.

Leave the middle of the sheet empty. Something is coming for that spot next time.

Was this easy? I don't think it's hard. But it does need to be done properly.

See you in two days.

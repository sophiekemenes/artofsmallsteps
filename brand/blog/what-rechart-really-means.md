There's a word lots of people misunderstand, even though we use it all the time (and I'd say satnavs get it a little bit wrong too).

Recharting. Or, as my satnav would put it: *recalculating.*

No wonder that when people hear it, a very specific picture pops into their head: you take a big eraser, rub out your life so far, and start being someone else. New job, new city, new haircut. A new trip, a few new skills, and I won't hold it against you if somewhere at the back of your mind there's a pottery class too, just in case. (Been there 😎)

But let me offer you a twist. The research – which I've been digging through quite a lot these past few weeks, half out of a researcher's curiosity, and the other half because it's very much on my mind right now – says something quite different.

It turns out that "reinvention" isn't a neatly defined concept in psychology at all. Researchers work with words like *transition*, *identity work*, *narrative identity*. What they have in common will surprise no one: every so often there comes a point in your life when the way you've always understood yourself no longer answers a fairly simple question.

**Who am I in this life that's happening right now?**

And that point, of course, is rarely a single event. Not necessarily a divorce or being made redundant or moving house (although it can be).

Very often it's simply that what you've built suddenly doesn't sit on you the way it used to. First just one small thing doesn't fit. Then another, and then, in no time at all, the whole shop's upside down, because it's coming at you from every direction.

One of the oldest findings in career research is that a transition is not an event but a process – and you're often well into it long before you even notice.

Then there's the question of identity, which for me is the most exciting part. For a long time we were lulled into believing (or lulled ourselves into believing) that adult identity gets built once and then it's done, like a three-door BRIMNES wardrobe, where there are always four mysterious screws left over at the end, and it's best not to know what they were for.

Whereas, whoops, it keeps taking shape all through adulthood. There's continuity and change in you at the same time – you want to keep the feeling that you're the same person, while life keeps bringing new roles, losses and possibilities.

So that's why my eye twitches a little at "find your new self" type advice. Recharting is very rarely about manufacturing a new you. It's much more about building a new, closer and, not least, accepting relationship with what's already there in you right now.

A previous profession, for example, wasn't just a job. It held skills, relationships, ways of thinking, problems you loved solving – and some you never want to see again. When you change career, you're not starting from zero. You bring your whole previous life with you, whether you want to or not. Feel free to swap in a previous relationship, or a thousand other things that shape a life.

And to make it a bit more personal, because although there's no guaranteed stage of life when this hits us, well: midlife is especially fertile ground. Not because you're obliged to have a crisis somewhere around forty or fifty, but because by then there's usually plenty to reinterpret.

There's a past, there's a professional identity, there are decisions that can't be undone. And at the same time there's a future too. The two together make for a rather special situation.

Maybe that's exactly why it's so hard to notice when it arrives. Because there's no official notification. You don't get a push message on your phone saying "support for your current identity will end soon, please update". Usually all you feel is that something that used to work doesn't quite work any more.

Which, you know, isn't necessarily a bad thing. Maybe your life has simply got to the point where the old map no longer shows every road.

So this is what I've worked out: life tosses you about, you fall, you get up, you create, you knock things down – and if you've outgrown your skin, you don't have to become a completely new person, because you're already pretty great as you are…

Instead, skilfully and with a gentle hand, you start reading your own story differently. The old failure doesn't simply stay a failure. An old job isn't ten wasted years. An interest you dropped isn't just "something you used to like".

It's raw material. Data. Ingredients – and if so far you've been making, say, a nice lecsó with them (a Hungarian pepper-and-tomato stew, the kind every family swears they make best), now you could make ANYTHING, just as delicious, or even better.

Think about it 😊 The same memories. The same experiences. Only the connections between them will be different.
